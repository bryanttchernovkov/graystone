# Graystone — bible

## Premise

**Graystone** is a fictional Midwest county seat of about thirty thousand people: a limestone
courthouse square, grain elevators by the rail spur, a river with one good bridge, a quarry
that gave the town its name and most of its buildings their color. Strip malls on the bypass,
bars on Front Street, a diner that opens at five. Present day. Nobody here is famous and
everybody knows everybody's truck.

For a decade the town slid: meth on the county roads, break-ins on Main, bar fights that ended
in the river. Then **the Mayor** — a woman who grew up here and came back to run it — took
office on one promise: *it ends*. With the county board behind her she **suspended the
traditional court docket** for misdemeanors and handed enforcement to a rebuilt **Graystone
County Sheriff's Department, staffed entirely by women**. The department doesn't file charges.
It **corrects**: on the spot, on the shoulder of the county road, behind the bar at closing —
or, for anyone who needs longer, with **a short stay in the department's own lockup**, rarely
more than a few days, from which men come out straighter than they went in. Crime is down.
Nobody in Graystone pretends to wonder why.

The department works out of **the old First Church**, a deconsecrated limestone Gothic Revival
church on the square that the county bought for a dollar. The nave is the bullpen; the
lockup is **the Undercroft**, the vaulted crypt beneath it.

**The player is always a man**, and always a newcomer to the department's attention. The first
hero is new in town; more heroes come later, each with his own reasons and his own slots and
dice. A run lasts as long as his time does.

### Voice (rules for every line of prose)

- **Plain and physical.** Say what happens, in the order it happens, with concrete nouns and
  verbs. Sex is written explicitly, in plain words, never in metaphor-fog.
- **One comparison per paragraph, at most**, and only a concrete, in-world one.
- **The wit lives in the women's mouths.** Dry, specific, Midwestern lines — understatement,
  politeness with an edge. Narration stays lean around them.
- **Banned tics:** *unhurried*, *economy*, *exactly* (as emphasis), *which tells you*,
  sentences that comment on the sentence before.
- **No history.** Results are fixed: never assume what he did, holds or knows.
- **The prompt promises the result.** If it turns sexual or punitive, the prompt says so.
- **He is careful, not cringing.** He notices and says yes; the women carry the scene.

### Second pass (after every batch of prose)

Write first, then reread the batch for these alone. They are the tics that make the prose read
machine-made; one is a style, a pattern of them is a tell.

- **No "the way you'd…" comparisons.** Say the thing. If a comparison earns its place it is
  concrete and in-world, and still one per paragraph.
- **No narrator verdicts.** Cut lines that tell him how to take it: *which is worse*, *and that's
  worse*, *it sounds like it costs her something*, *you'd give a lot to…*. Show what she does;
  the reader decides.
- **No kicker on every result.** Most results end on an action or her line, not on a quip or a
  one-line moral. A closer has to add something the scene hasn't already said.
- **No staged pauses.** *Long enough to…*, *longer than she needs to*, *for a long moment*: once a
  storyline, at most.
- **No "not X, not Y" and no "X. Not Y."** State what it is.
- **Break the rhythm.** No runs of *Then…* openings, no strings of *and… and… and*, no group of
  three where two or four is true.
- **One voice per woman.** Each has her own words for control (Anderson's phone and "babe",
  Ashby's ranks and orders, Seo's notebook and nicknames, the Signora's counts). "I'll know" is
  Juno's, because she can see his screen; not every woman makes him say it again, louder.
- **A detail is used once.** A description of a woman, a boot or a sock appears in one scene;
  the next scene finds a different detail. Search the storyline for repeats before committing.

## The world's shape

Graystone grows **iteratively, storyline by storyline**: a deputy, a gang, a bar, a case — each
added with its locations, cards and art, not generated in one pass. The first anchor is the
**First Church station** and the **Undercroft** beneath it.

Like Precinct 69, **event scenes are locations**: the shoulder of the county road with a
cruiser behind you, a cell, the back of a patrol car — entered because something moved you
there, always with a way back into the world. **People live at their places**; a rare few may
follow the player as companion cards.

**Places and events are kept apart** (authoring convention; players never see IDs or maps):

- **Places** are where you can walk. They get place IDs (`main-street-north`, `heights`) and
  sit on walkable maps (`town`, interiors linked by portals).
- **Events** are scenes something moved you into. They get **`<storyline>-<event>` IDs**
  (`undercroft-charm-warm`, `undercroft-trusted-release`), sit on **their storyline's map**,
  and are reached only by `move.to`. A header comment says what sends you there. Their display
  `name` may be a place ("The First Church steps"): names needn't be unique, so another
  storyline can stage its own event on the same steps.
- Place IDs stay free for the real place, should it ever become walkable.

## Rules configuration

`universe.yaml` holds **only the universe-wide layer**; storylines bring their own cards and
tags. Nothing in it is shaped around one storyline.

- **Categories:** TALK, WORK, TAKE, RISK, LOOK, DEAL, plus GO for exits. There is **no spicy
  category**: heat can come through any of them, and the word decides (WORK-POLISH,
  DEAL-TERMS, TALK-ASK). DEAL covers terms, trades, bargains and agreements. What the terms
  are is the surprise.
- **Trackers:** Time (max 12), Body and Nerve (max 6). **A run is as long as its Time.**
  Results and cards can give Time back, and a run goes on until the mission is done or the
  day runs out.
- **Currency:** Cash only.
- **Card kinds:**
  - **hero** — centre; one per playable man, each with his own slots and powers. Dice faces are
    universe-wide, so heroes differ through slots, powers, boost and starting cash.
  - **mission** — supply.
  - **gear** — grid; things he can use himself.
  - **restraint** — grid, forced; things put on him: cuffs, prison chains, gags, chastity cages.
  - **favor** — grid; standing with a woman or a faction. He places dice on it to support her,
    and it pays off in a benefit.
  - **fetish** — grid; a taste he has picked up (Boot Fetish, Uniform Fetish). Its tag
    (`boots`, `uniform`) is what other storylines `require` to open their doors.
  - **dossier** — grid, forced; what the town keeps on him: his Criminal Record, a gang's
    tally. It's in the grid because dice drive it (a record that fills with counts outside and
    is worked off inside).
  - **mark** — grid, forced; something done to him that shows: Anderson's Sharpie on his

- **Words don't give the scene away.** The player sees only category and word before
